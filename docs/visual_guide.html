01

The core idea: fear is overpriced

Every S&P 500 option has a price, and buried inside that price is the market's guess at how much the index will move before the option expires. That guess — implied volatility — tends to run higher than the volatility that actually shows up. The gap between the two is the volatility risk premium, and it's the entire reason this strategy exists.

Plain English

Think of VIX like the price of car insurance. Insurance companies don't price a policy at the exact expected cost of an accident — they price in a margin, because they're the ones on the hook if something goes wrong. Options are the same: the people selling them (index option market makers, effectively) charge a bit more than the "fair" price of the risk, because they need to be compensated for occasionally getting hit with a real crash.

This project is built around being the insurance company instead of the person buying the policy — collecting that margin systematically, in a way that's designed so no single bad month can wipe out the account.

The Math

VIX is (roughly) the market's model-free estimate of 30-day annualized S&P 500 volatility, derived from a basket of option prices. Realized volatility over the same horizon is computed directly from what the index actually did:

RV= √252 × std(ln(Pt/Pt−1))
Pt
SPX closing price on day t
Pt−1
SPX closing price the prior trading day
std(·)
standard deviation, taken over the trailing 21-day window ending at t
252
trading days per year — the annualization factor

The volatility risk premium is just VIX − RV. It's been documented across methodologies going back to Coval & Shumway (2001) and Bakshi & Kapadia (2003) — see docs/research/vrp_literature_review.md for the full citation trail.

02

The trade: an iron condor

You can't just "sell the VIX." The actual trade is four option legs at once, structured so the maximum loss is known and fixed the moment the trade opens — this is not naked put selling.

Iron condor payoff at expiration
Hover a zone for what's happening there
Plain English

You're selling a put and a call that are both moderately close to the current price (the "short" strikes, around 20-delta — roughly a 1-in-5 chance of finishing in the money), which is where most of the premium is. Then you buy a further-out put and call (the "long" wings, around 5-delta) purely as insurance for yourself.

If the index stays roughly where it is, all four options expire worthless and you keep the full credit. If it moves a lot in either direction, you start losing — but the wings mean the loss stops growing past a certain point. You know your worst case before you ever place the trade.

The Math

Max profit = net credit received. Max loss = (wing width − net credit), and it's the same formula whether the index crashes or melts up:

Lmax=(Kshort−Klong)−credit
Lmax
maximum possible loss on the trade — known and fixed at entry
Kshort
the strike you sold (the short put or call, closer to the money)
Klong
the strike you bought (the long put or call — the wing, further out)
credit
net premium collected when the trade was opened

Because the loss is bounded, this position is Kelly-tractable — the Kelly criterion needs a known loss size to compute a bet size, which a naked put (unbounded loss) can't give you. That's the whole reason src/risk/sizing.py can eventually compute a real position size here.

03

The signal: only trade when fear is expensive

Selling premium every single month regardless of conditions is one strategy. This project does something more specific: only opens a trade when implied volatility is elevated relative to its own recent history — the "IV rank" filter.

IV rank over time, with the three thresholds being tested
252-day trailing lookback · shaded bands show where each threshold would fire
IV rank (0–100) Thresholds: 50 / 70 / 80
Plain English

IV rank asks one question: compared to the last year, is implied volatility high or low right now? A VIX of 20 means something completely different in a calm year (that would be unusually high) versus a turbulent one (that might be unusually low). IV rank puts every day on the same 0–100 scale so the signal adapts to the current regime instead of using one fixed VIX number forever.

The chart makes it visible: the same VIX level in early 2020 vs. mid-2022 produces a very different rank, because "recent history" itself shifted.

The Math
IVrank(t)=100× IVt − Mint Maxt − Mint
IVt
implied volatility (VIX) on day t
Mint, Maxt
the minimum and maximum IV over the trailing 252 trading days ending at t — never including data from after day t

The window is trailing — day t's rank only ever uses data through day t. This sounds obvious, but it's the single easiest way to accidentally cheat in a backtest: if a "252-day window" quietly includes a few days from the future, the signal looks smarter than it could ever have been traded live. Every function in this project that touches history gets a specific test for this (tests/test_iv_rank.py, tests/test_backtest_engine.py).

Why these numbers aren't the final answer
  • No real options chain data yet. Every trade is priced with Black-Scholes off VIX as a single flat volatility — real SPX puts trade at a noticeably higher implied vol than calls at the same delta (skew), which this proxy can't see. That understates the credit collected on the put wing.
  • One contract sized against a $1,000 placeholder account. Worst-case single trades ran — against that $1,000 figure — a clear sign that real position sizing (src/risk/sizing.py, still a stub) has to exist before any of this is tradeable, not just nice to have.
  • Hold-to-expiration only. No early profit-taking or defensive exits modeled yet (v2 in the spec).
