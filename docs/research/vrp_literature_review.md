# The Volatility Risk Premium and Defined-Risk Options Selling: An Academic and Institutional Literature Review for an SPX/SPY Iron-Condor Project

## TL;DR
- The volatility risk premium (VRP) — implied volatility persistently exceeding subsequently realized volatility — is one of the best-documented anomalies in finance, confirmed across four decades by peer-reviewed work (Bakshi-Kapadia 2003, Coval-Shumway 2001, Carr-Wu 2009, Bondarenko 2003/2014) and by CBOE's own benchmark indices; a rigorous, honest student write-up can credibly claim the edge is real but modest, negatively skewed, and prone to crowding.
- Defined-risk structures (iron condors like CBOE's CNDR) trade away return for dramatically lower volatility and tail risk: over the 29½-year Black-Szado study period (mid-1986 to end-2015), CNDR had the lowest annualized standard deviation (7.23%) of the six CBOE options-selling indices and a ~19% max drawdown, versus ~51% for the S&P 500 — exactly the trade-off a small, capital-constrained student account should want.
- Every well-documented short-vol blow-up (LJM, XIV/Volmageddon, Allianz Structured Alpha, Malachite, Parplus/Ronin) shares the same root causes — leverage, undefined/insufficiently hedged tails, oversizing, and mandate drift — none of which are inherent to a small, unlevered, defined-risk iron condor; the danger is in implementation, not in the premium itself.

## Key Findings

1. **The academic case is strong but nuanced.** Multiple independent methodologies — delta-hedged option returns, raw option/straddle returns, model-free variance-swap replication, and stochastic-dominance bounds — all converge on a negative market volatility/variance risk premium (i.e., option sellers are compensated). But every serious paper stresses the premium is compensation for bearing crash risk, is negatively skewed, and spikes against sellers precisely in market stress.

2. **Reputable managers treat VRP as "selling insurance," and unanimously emphasize sizing and tail management.** AQR documents that a naive delta-hedged 5% OTM S&P put-selling strategy earned a Sharpe of 0.68 (1996–2016), but repeatedly warns that allocations "should be sized appropriately to reflect this tail risk."

3. **CBOE's index family quantifies the risk/return spectrum.** Naked-ish ATM sellers (PUT) and OTM call writers (BXMD) earned the highest returns (~9.5–10.7% annualized) but with equity-like drawdowns (~35–43%); defined-risk CNDR earned less but with the lowest volatility (7.23%) and smallest drawdown (~19%).

4. **IV-rank/VIX-conditioned entry has direct peer-reviewed support.** Malkiel, Rinaudo & Saha (2018, *Journal of Derivatives*) show selling options only at elevated VIX beat both the market and continuous option-selling on absolute and risk-adjusted terms over 1990–2018.

5. **The blow-ups are a study in what NOT to do**, and they validate the defined-risk design rather than indict premium selling generally.

## Details

### 1. Core academic literature on the variance/volatility risk premium

**Coval & Shumway (2001), "Expected Option Returns," *Journal of Finance* 56(3), 983–1009.** A foundational paper. Method: examines raw returns of S&P 100/500 index options and, critically, zero-beta ATM straddles (constructed to have no market exposure). Findings: expected call returns exceed the underlying and rise with strike; put returns are below the risk-free rate; and crucially, zero-beta at-the-money straddles lose ~3% per week. Because a zero-beta straddle should earn the risk-free rate under CAPM, these systematic losses imply an additional priced factor — systematic stochastic volatility. Period: 1986–1995 (weekly). This established that "something beyond market risk" is priced in options.

**Bakshi & Kapadia (2003), "Delta-Hedged Gains and the Negative Market Volatility Risk Premium," *Review of Financial Studies* 16(2), 527–566.** The most-cited direct test. Method: forms delta-hedged option portfolios (long option, dynamically hedged with the underlying) so that returns isolate exposure to volatility rather than direction. Under a stochastic-volatility model, the mean delta-hedged gain maps directly to the sign/magnitude of the volatility risk premium. Findings: (1) delta-hedged gains are negative (sellers profit); (2) underperformance is smaller for away-from-money options; (3) it is larger when volatility is high; (4) it survives controls for jump fear, so it reflects a genuine volatility risk premium, not just crash fear. Instrument: S&P 500 index options. A companion 2003 paper extended the analysis to individual equity options.

**Carr & Wu (2009), "Variance Risk Premiums," *Review of Financial Studies* 22(3), 1311–1341** (widely circulated as a 2004 working paper). Method: a "model-free" approach — synthesizes the variance swap rate from a portfolio of options (so the risk-neutral expected variance requires no parametric model), then defines the variance risk premium as realized variance minus this synthetic swap rate. Data: 5 stock indices and 35 individual stocks. Findings: the S&P 500 variance risk premium is strongly negative and large; it is not well explained by standard Fama-French/momentum factors, implying variance is its own priced risk factor. This paper is the methodological backbone for modern VIX-style variance measurement.

**Bondarenko (2003/2014), "Why Are Put Options So Expensive?" *Quarterly Journal of Finance* 4(1), 1–50.** Method: a genuinely "model-free" statistical test using stochastic-dominance / a general class of equilibrium pricing kernels, explicitly robust to the "Peso problem" (rare-disaster selection bias) and to incorrect investor beliefs. Finding: the "overpriced puts puzzle" — historical S&P 500 put returns are too negative (puts too expensive) to be reconciled with any model in a broad class, including CAPM and the Rubinstein (1976) model. This is the key citation for the claim that put richness is a robust anomaly, not merely an artifact of not having observed a big-enough crash. (This is the same Oleg Bondarenko who later authored the CBOE VRP white papers already referenced in the project.)

**Bollerslev, Tauchen & Zhou (2009), "Expected Stock Returns and Variance Risk Premia," *RFS* 22(11), 4463–4492.** Method: defines VRP as the difference between model-free implied variance and expected realized variance (using high-frequency intraday data). Finding: the VRP predicts aggregate stock returns, especially at the quarterly horizon, where it dominates the P/E ratio, default spread, and consumption-wealth ratio. Relevance to the project: it shows VRP is time-varying and highest when future returns are highest — an economically motivated reason that conditioning entries on elevated implied vol could help.

**Points of agreement/disagreement and critiques.**
- *Agreement:* All the above agree the sign of the premium is negative (sellers paid) for index options, and that it concentrates in downside/OTM puts.
- *Disagreement/nuance:* Coval-Shumway attribute losses to a generic stochastic-volatility factor; Bakshi-Kapadia isolate volatility risk from jump risk; later pricing-kernel work finds non-monotonic ("U-shaped") kernels implying the strike/return relationship is not as clean as Coval-Shumway's monotone story.
- *Replication/decay concerns:* The premium is real but data-mined windows flatter it. Practitioner and academic work note the trade became crowded in the low-vol 2010s; the CBOE iron-condor index in particular was roughly flat post-2010 after strong pre-2010 returns (see §3). Honest write-ups should treat pre-2010 and post-2010 backtests separately.

### 2. How reputable firms describe and structure VRP strategies

**AQR Capital Management — "Understanding the Volatility Risk Premium" (May 2018; Ang, Israelov, Sullivan, Tummala).** The clearest institutional primer. Framing: VRP is "compensation that investors earn for providing protection against market losses" — literally selling insurance — and it exists partly because investors overestimate crash probability. AQR cites the Yale U.S. Crash Confidence Survey (data April 1989–December 2016): roughly two-thirds of both institutional and retail investors on average believe there is a greater than 10% chance of a catastrophic crash within the next six months, versus a historical frequency of only about 1%. Empirics (data January 4, 1996 through December 31, 2016, S&P 500): a continuously rolled 5% OTM protective-put *buyer* cut return from 5.1% to 1.8% (Sharpe 0.32 → 0.14) — protection is expensive. The mirror-image *seller* of the same put, delta-hedged daily, earned 1.5% return on 2.2% volatility for a Sharpe of 0.68 with beta 0.04 and max drawdown −10%. Risk management: the strategy's core risk is "sudden, large market movements" (both up and down — March 2000 and September 2008 are their worst months), and standalone allocations "should be sized appropriately to reflect this tail risk." AQR also runs a registered mutual fund (AQR Volatility Risk Premium Fund) and warns leverage makes NAV "more volatile and sensitive to market movements."

**AQR — Israelov & Nielsen, "Covered Calls Uncovered" (*Financial Analysts Journal* 71(6), 2015) and Israelov, Klein & Tummala, "Covering the World" (*Journal of Risk*, 2018).** Crucial for honest attribution: they decompose a covered-call / short-vol strategy into three exposures — passive equity, dynamic equity ("equity reversal" market-timing), and short volatility. Finding: the pure short-volatility component had a realized Sharpe near 1.0 but contributed less than 10% of the covered call's risk; the uncompensated equity-reversal timing exposure contributed about one-quarter of risk for little reward. Implication for the student project: the "clean" VRP is a small, high-Sharpe sliver; most of the risk/return in naive option selling is just equity beta in disguise. Isolating VRP requires hedging the directional exposure.

**Neuberger Berman.** Runs a multibillion-dollar S&P 500 put-writing business (Neuberger Option Strategy ETF / former PutWrite fund; PM Derek Devens, CIO Erik Knutzen). Public framing: "underwriting market risk—by writing puts on the S&P 500 Index—can be a capital-efficient strategy… for income," and they explicitly lean into elevated VIX ("taking advantage of the elevated VIX to capture higher premiums"). Their marketed structure — cash/Treasury-collateralized puts benchmarked to the CBOE PutWrite methodology — is the un-levered, fully-collateralized discipline that distinguishes survivors from blow-ups.

**Man Group / Man AHL.** Man AHL's research emphasizes volatility scaling / volatility targeting (lever down when vol is high, up when low) to make return distributions more symmetric and improve Sharpe. Their academic advisory work stresses that "volatility is not the same as risk" once investors care about skew and tail risk — a direct caution that Sharpe ratios understate short-vol danger.

**LJM (pre-collapse marketing) as contrast.** LJM's prospectus stated an objective of "capital appreciation and capital preservation with low correlation to the broader U.S. equity market" — while the fund actually sold *naked* puts on S&P 500 futures with leverage and above-average margin. This gap between "preservation" marketing and leveraged, undefined-risk reality is the canonical governance/mandate-drift failure; the CFTC and SEC filed civil charges and the chief risk officer settled with an industry ban.

### 3. CBOE (and exchange) options-benchmark index family

CBOE publishes 30+ strategy benchmarks. The most relevant, with published methodologies:

- **BXM (S&P 500 BuyWrite):** long S&P 500 + sell 1-month ATM SPX call. The original (2002) benchmark.
- **BXMD (30-Delta BuyWrite):** long S&P 500 + sell 1-month ~30-delta OTM call — collects less premium per option but retains more upside; historically the highest-returning of the family.
- **PUT (PutWrite):** hold Treasuries + sell cash-secured ATM SPX puts. Fully collateralized (no leverage) by construction.
- **WPUT / PUTY:** weekly and 2%-OTM variants of PUT.
- **CMBO (Covered Combo):** long S&P 500 + sell ATM put + sell 2% OTM call (put collateralized by T-bills, call by the equity).
- **BFLY (Iron Butterfly):** sell ATM put + ATM call, buy OTM put + call wings.
- **CNDR (Iron Condor):** sell ~0.20-delta OTM put + ~0.20-delta OTM call, buy ~0.05-delta OTM put + call wings, hold T-bills. No active management, monthly AM-settled roll. This is the project's primary benchmark.
- Others: BXY (2% OTM BuyWrite), CLL/CLLZ (collars), PPUT (5% put protection), RXM (risk reversal), plus VIX-based (VPD, VPN, VXTH) and Russell 2000 variants (PUTR, BXR).

**Long-run performance (Wilshire Analytics for CBOE, June 1986 – Dec 2018, total-return, before transaction costs/taxes):**

**CNDR and BFLY specifics (Black & Szado, "Performance Analysis of CBOE S&P 500 Options-Selling Indices," CBOE, Feb 23, 2016; 29½-year period, mid-1986–end-2015).** Verbatim from CBOE's announcement: "The indexes with the lowest annualized standard deviation were the CNDR (7.23 percent) and PUT (10.16 percent) indexes," while the highest-return indices over the period were "the BXMD (10.66 percent) and the PUT (10.13 percent)." On tail risk, over July 1986–December 2015 the S&P 500 posted 15 months of losses worse than 6%, while "the CNDR Index logged 10 months of losses worse than 6 percent and the BFLY index two months of losses worse than 6 percent." CBOE's 2021 update ("Volatility Management with Cboe's BFLY and CNDR Indices") reports the worst peak-to-trough drawdown at approximately 19% for CNDR and 47.1% for BFLY, versus ~51% for the S&P 500. The structural reason CNDR was not among the top return performers: it spends part of the premium it collects buying the 0.05-delta protective wings, which caps net credit relative to naked/ATM sellers like PUT and BXMD. Note also that CNDR's returns were front-loaded — strong before 2010 and roughly flat in the low-volatility 2010s, a crowding/regime caveat the write-up should flag. (CNDR's exact CAGR, Sharpe and Sortino from the full study could not be verified from a primary CBOE source in this research — see Caveats.)

**The implied volatility risk premium itself (Wilshire/CBOE).** Average VIX 1990–2018 was 19.3% versus 15.5% average 30-day realized — an average implied/realized ratio of ~129% — and implied exceeded realized in all but one of the past 21 years (2008 the exception). Average BXM monthly gross premium was ~1.7% of the underlying. This is the empirical engine of the whole strategy and corroborates the project's already-known 19.3% vs 15.1% figure.

### 4. Literature specific to iron condors, strike/wing selection, sizing, and IV-timing

**IV-rank/VIX timing (the project's entry signal) — the strongest single citation:** Malkiel, Rinaudo & Saha (2018), "Option Writing: Using VIX to Improve Returns," *Journal of Derivatives* 26(2), 38–49 (DOI 10.3905/jod.2018.26.2.038). Verbatim from the abstract: "we propose a simple conditional strategy in which one sells options at elevated levels of the VIX. Using data from 1990 through 2018, we find that this conditional strategy outperforms both the market and continuous option-selling strategies on an absolute and risk-adjusted basis." Lead author Burton G. Malkiel (of *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*) is the Chemical Bank Chairman's Professor of Economics, Emeritus, at Princeton, which adds citation weight. Bollerslev-Tauchen-Zhou (2009) supplies the theoretical mechanism (VRP is time-varying and predicts returns).

**Position sizing / risk-of-ruin frameworks.** The Kelly criterion (Kelly 1956) is the standard reference; practitioners uniformly recommend fractional Kelly (half or quarter) because full Kelly assumes the win rate and payoff are known exactly, and overestimating the edge is "how accounts blow up." A key caveat directly relevant here: Kelly assumes a *known, bounded* loss per trade — which is exactly why defined-risk iron condors (bounded max loss = wing width − net credit) are Kelly-tractable while naked puts (open-ended loss) are not. A 2025 arXiv working paper ("Sizing the Risk: Kelly, VIX, and Hybrid Approaches in Put-Writing on Index Options") tests Kelly sizing, VIX-regime scaling, and a hybrid on SPX weekly options and finds ultra-short-dated, far-OTM options with volatility-aware sizing deliver the best risk-adjusted results — but this is a working paper, not peer-reviewed.

**Strike/delta and wing-width selection.** Rigorous academic treatment is thin; the CBOE CNDR methodology (0.20-delta shorts, 0.05-delta wings) is the most authoritative "reference" design. The bulk of specific delta/wing/DTE optimization evidence comes from practitioner backtests (tastylive, Option Alpha, DTR Trading, Options Trading IQ) — acceptable as secondary sources but to be flagged as non-peer-reviewed and often in-sample. Broadly consistent practitioner findings: lower-delta shorts (8–16 delta) raise win rates but shrink credit; wider wings raise theta/credit but increase capital at risk and approach a naked strangle; managing at ~50% of max profit and/or at 21 DTE historically improved Sharpe versus holding to expiration. For a student project these should be framed as hypotheses to test in your own backtest, not established facts.

### 5. Other real-world cautionary tales (beyond LJM and the general XIV/Volmageddon episode)

- **XIV / Volmageddon (Feb 5, 2018):** The VelocityShares Daily Inverse VIX Short-Term ETN (Credit Suisse). The VIX rose from about 17.3 (Feb 2) to about 37.3 (Feb 5) — a one-day surge (+116%) that triggered the ETN's "acceleration event" (intraday indicative value fell below 20% of the prior close). XIV lost ~96% (NAV reported at $4.22, versus ~$99 the prior close), SVXY ~91%, and VMIN ~87% — while the S&P 500 fell only ~4.1%. Credit Suisse announced termination on Feb 6; the last trade was Feb 20, 2018. Lesson: leveraged, path-dependent, reflexive products with automatic acceleration clauses can go to near-zero on a single-day vol spike. A product-design and leverage failure, not a defined-risk-selling failure.
- **Allianz Structured Alpha (Q1 2020):** Marketed to pensions as hedged and providing "consistent, uncorrelated returns regardless of the direction of equities and volatility," these funds instead ran a net-short-volatility book. Per DOJ/SEC (May 17, 2022), the funds held more than $11 billion of assets and lost about $7 billion in February–March 2020 (individual funds lost between 49% and 97% of value in Q1 2020). Allianz Global Investors U.S. pleaded guilty to securities fraud and agreed to pay more than $6 billion (roughly $463 million in DOJ forfeiture, $3.2 billion in restitution, and $2.3 billion in penalties); the SEC charged lead portfolio manager Gregoire (Greg) Tournant with orchestrating a multi-year scheme to mislead investors. Lesson: mandate drift and inadequate tail hedging dressed up as "always hedged."
- **Malachite Capital (2020):** A ~$600M fund run by ex-Goldman equity-derivatives traders; it returned roughly 22%/21% in 2016/2017 selling leveraged low-delta puts and variance-swap caps, then collapsed in the March 2020 vol spike. Lesson: multi-year winning streaks in short-vol are not evidence of safety — they are the calm before the tail.
- **Parplus Partners / Ronin Capital (2020):** Parplus's blow-up cascaded into Ronin Capital (its backer) failing to meet CME margin, forcing CME to auction Ronin's portfolio. Lesson: counterparty/margin/liquidity linkages turn one fund's tail loss into systemic contagion.
- **AIMCo (Alberta, 2020):** The public pension's volatility unit lost on the order of C$2.1 billion on a short-vol trade and shut the unit. Lesson: even sophisticated, well-capitalized institutions mis-sized this exposure.

The common thread across all five plus LJM: **leverage, undefined or under-hedged tails, oversizing relative to capital, and marketing/mandate that hid the true short-tail exposure.** A small, unlevered, fully-defined-risk iron condor with disciplined sizing is specifically engineered to avoid every one of these failure modes — precisely the argument the project's write-up should make.

## Recommendations

**Framing for a transfer/portfolio audience (staged):**
1. **Lead with the honest thesis.** State that the VRP is real and multiply-documented (cite Bakshi-Kapadia 2003, Coval-Shumway 2001, Carr-Wu 2009, Bondarenko 2003/2014), but is compensation for bearing crash risk, negatively skewed, and subject to crowding/decay. This intellectual honesty is what impresses admissions/portfolio reviewers.
2. **Justify the defined-risk structure explicitly.** Use the CBOE data: CNDR's 7.23% volatility and ~19% max drawdown vs. ~51% for the S&P 500 is the quantitative argument that iron condors trade return for survivability — the correct objective for small capital. Benchmark your backtest against CNDR.
3. **Justify the IV-rank entry with a real citation.** Malkiel-Rinaudo-Saha (2018) is your anchor; in your own backtest, explicitly test conditional (high-IV-rank) vs. unconditional entry and report whether you replicate their result — replication attempts read as rigor.
4. **Adopt Kelly-aware, fractional sizing.** Because your max loss per condor is bounded (wing width − credit), compute a fractional-Kelly (≤ half-Kelly) or fixed 1–2%-of-capital-at-risk rule; document risk-of-ruin. State a hard portfolio-heat cap (e.g., total defined risk ≤ a fixed % of equity).
5. **Pre-commit to management rules and a tail plan.** Decide in advance on profit-taking (e.g., 50% of max profit), defensive/exit triggers, avoiding earnings/known events, and NEVER adding leverage or "rolling for a credit" into a losing tail (the LJM/Malachite error). Paper-trade the full ruleset before any live capital.

**Benchmarks/thresholds that should change the plan:**
- If your out-of-sample (post-2010, and especially 2018 & 2020) backtest shows the conditional strategy does *not* beat CNDR on Sharpe/Sortino, treat the edge as decayed and reduce size or shelve live trading.
- If a single defined-risk loss would exceed your risk-of-ruin tolerance at your account size, the position is too big regardless of signal.
- If realized transaction costs/slippage on SPY (or a 1-lot SPX) materially erode the thin net credit, that is disqualifying — the CBOE indices ignore costs, so your live edge will be smaller than any backtest.

## Caveats
- **All CBOE/Wilshire index statistics are hypothetical, gross of transaction costs and taxes, and partly back-tested before each index's launch date.** Real implementation (especially in SPY with retail commissions/slippage and no perfect AM settlement) will underperform. CBOE funded both the Wilshire and Black-Szado studies — a conflict to note.
- **Sharpe ratios systematically flatter short-vol strategies** because returns are negatively skewed and fat-tailed (Wilshire reports skew of −1.11 to −2.10 for these indices). Report Sortino, Stutzer, max drawdown, and worst-month alongside Sharpe.
- **CNDR's exact CAGR, Sharpe, and Sortino from the 1986–2015 Black-Szado study could not be verified from primary sources during this research** (the full study PDF was not retrievable). The confirmed CNDR figures are the 7.23% annualized standard deviation (CBOE, Feb 2016) and the ~19% max drawdown (CBOE 2021 update). Verify CAGR/Sharpe from a current CBOE Global Indices CNDR fact sheet before publishing a specific number.
- **Some cited practitioner sources (tastylive, Option Alpha, DTR Trading, Options Trading IQ, and the 2025 arXiv sizing paper) are not peer-reviewed** and often report in-sample results; use them only as secondary/hypothesis-generating support, clearly labeled.
- **The premium is regime-dependent and appears to have compressed in the crowded 2010s** (CNDR roughly flat post-2010). Do not present pre-2010 backtest returns as representative of the forward opportunity; separate the eras.
- **Firm marketing materials (AQR, Neuberger Berman, Man Group) describe strategies these firms sell** and should be read as informed but not disinterested.
